Relapse Prevention

Recovery is an ongoing process, and returning to substance use is not always a sudden or isolated event. Changes in emotions, daily routines, thoughts, and support systems can signal increased risk before substance use occurs.

This week, you'll return to Harbor Point to explore how relapse can develop, recognize warning signs, and examine strategies clients can use to manage cravings and respond to high-risk situations.

You'll also practice using HALT, supporting self-care and help-seeking, and considering how recovery needs change over time. Through realistic scenarios, you'll learn how to respond without judgment while working within your CDCA role.

First, learn to recognize how the relapse process can begin well before a person returns to substance use.

Relapse Can Develop Over Time

Recovery affects physical, emotional, behavioral, interpersonal, and social parts of life. Relapse is often described using three patterns. These are useful educational concepts, not a tool for predicting exactly what any one person will do.

🌧️Emotional relapse

A person may not be thinking about using, but isolation, neglected self-care, missed meetings, or increased stress can make recovery harder.

💭Mental relapse

The person may feel torn between recovery and substance use. Cravings, idealizing prior use, bargaining, and planning can emerge.

🔄Physical relapse

A person returns to substance use. Respond with compassion, immediate attention to safety, and connection to appropriate treatment support.

A person's experiences may not follow a fixed sequence. Recognize what the client reports rather than assigning a label automatically.

What Do You Notice?

A client says, “I haven't thought about using again, but I've stopped going to meetings, barely sleep, and don't feel like talking to anyone.” The client is still attending treatment appointments.

Which response best supports the client?

Supporting Awareness Without Judgment

👀Notice changes

Pay attention to isolation, disrupted routines, cravings, and shifts in coping without assuming why they occur.

💬Explore together

Use open-ended questions, empathic listening, and collaborative problem-solving rather than accusations.

🤝Connect support

Work with the qualified treatment team to address risks, adjust supports, and identify next steps within your CDCA responsibilities.

Warning Signs Are Signals, Not Verdicts

Maria's guidance

“Relapse prevention starts with noticing changes and asking supportive questions. One warning sign does not mean a client will return to substance use. Our job is to help clients recognize patterns and connect with support.”

Many warning signs can be identified in a client's daily routines, relationships, thoughts, and ability to manage distress. Look for changes over time, and ask what those changes mean to the client.

🧭Motivation and engagement

Reduced interest in recovery activities, skipping meetings, or no longer reaching out for help.

🧰Coping and cravings

Difficulty coping with stressful events, increased cravings, or not seeking support when upset.

🤝Support and connection

Feeling disconnected, limited support, increased isolation, or returning to relationships associated with prior substance use.

🏙️People, places, and beliefs

Spending time where substances are being used, remembering substance use only positively, or minimizing consequences.

🌿Well-being

Unaddressed mental health concerns, disrupted sleep and nutrition, and growing feelings of boredom or distress.

A warning sign invites curiosity and a conversation, not a diagnosis, accusation, or prediction.

Look for Patterns, Not Labels

Individual circumstances affect how warning signs appear. Consider what each pattern might suggest and what supportive next step would help.

📆

Changes in routine

A person who usually attends peer-support meetings has missed several and stopped answering calls.

Notice: Reduced participation and contact with support.

💭

Changes in thinking

A person says they miss the “good parts” of using and questions whether one drink would matter.

Notice: Idealizing past use and bargaining.

🌙

Changes in well-being

A person has difficulty sleeping, feels overwhelmed, and struggles to use familiar coping skills.

Notice: Distress and difficulty maintaining self-care.

These are examples of possible risk patterns. A CDCA can listen, ask open-ended questions, share observations, and notify the qualified treatment team as appropriate.

Cravings Are Real, and They Can Be Managed

Cravings can feel intense, but they do not last forever. Clients may benefit from practical strategies that help them slow down, get support, and choose a safer next step instead of reacting automatically.

Maria's reminder

“When a client talks about cravings, we don’t need to panic. We can help them pause, notice what is happening, and reconnect with strategies that support recovery.”

Coping With Cravings

⏳Delay

Help clients remember that cravings rise and fall. Delaying action—even for a short time—can create space to use other coping skills.

🧭Distract or Refocus

Encourage activities that shift attention: walking, journaling, showering, listening to music, or doing something structured.

🤝Reach Out

Calling a sponsor, counselor, trusted friend, or support person can reduce isolation and strengthen accountability.

🚪Leave High-Risk Situations

If possible, help clients identify when stepping away from a person, place, or event may reduce risk.

📝Use a Plan

A coping plan can include warning signs, helpful supports, preferred coping tools, and steps to take if the craving continues.

💗Respond Without Shame

Cravings do not mean failure. A nonjudgmental response can help clients stay engaged and ask for help sooner.

High-Risk Situations

Cravings often become stronger in certain situations. Thinking ahead can help clients respond more safely.

📍Places

Specific neighborhoods, stores, bars, or homes may be closely tied to past substance use.

👥People

Contact with people connected to past use may increase pressure, memories, or bargaining thoughts.

🌀Emotions

Stress, anger, sadness, boredom, or even excitement can raise vulnerability when coping skills are limited.

🕒Time Patterns

Some clients notice cravings at certain times, such as evenings, weekends, or after work.

🛠️Preparation

A coping plan works best when it is specific: who to call, where to go, and what to do next.

👂CDCA Role

CDCAs can support planning, reinforce coping tools, and communicate concerns to the qualified treatment team.

🗺️Plan

📞Reach Out

Encourage support from the treatment team, sponsors, peers, family, or other recovery supports rather than facing cravings alone.

Maria's reminder

“Helping a client prepare for cravings is not about controlling them. It’s about helping them recognize risk, use supports, and stay connected to recovery.”

HALT: Check the Basics

A craving can become harder to manage when a client’s basic needs are not being met. The HALT reminder encourages a client to pause and ask: Am I Hungry, Angry, Lonely, or Tired? These states may make coping more difficult, but they are not diagnoses or proof that a return to use will happen.

Maria’s reminder

“A brief check-in about basic needs can help us understand what the client needs right now. HALT is a starting point for a conversation, not the whole relapse prevention plan.”

Hungry, Angry, Lonely, or Tired?

🥗H · Hungry

Skipping meals, long stretches without food, or inadequate nutrition may affect mood, energy, and coping.

🌋A · Angry

Frustration, conflict, or unaddressed emotions can make a stressful moment harder to navigate.

🫂L · Lonely

Isolation or feeling disconnected from supportive people may make coping more difficult.

🌙T · Tired

Poor sleep and exhaustion can reduce patience, concentration, and coping capacity.

🔎Notice Patterns

Ask which needs may be present and what helps this particular client feel steadier.

🧰Choose a Next Step

Link the need to a realistic action and the client’s existing recovery supports.

Self-Care Is Part of Relapse Prevention

Self-care does not need to be elaborate. Consistent routines and supportive relationships can lower avoidable stress and help clients respond to cravings with more options.

🍽️Food and Hydration

Notice whether meals, snacks, and water are accessible and part of the client’s daily routine.

🛏️Rest and Sleep

Identify realistic routines that support rest and address barriers to adequate sleep.

📱Connection

Identify trusted people, recovery groups, peers, or professionals the client can contact.

🚶Healthy Activity

A walk, exercise, reading, cleaning, or another meaningful activity may help the client shift attention during a craving.

🗣️Express Emotions

Help the client name frustration or stress and explore a safe way to respond rather than ignoring the feeling.

🤝Practical Support

Ask about access to food, safe housing, transportation, and other resources; collaborate with the qualified team as needed.

Maria’s reminder

“Support a small step the client chooses. If a basic need is difficult to meet, help connect them with resources rather than treating it as a lack of motivation.”

Five Rules of Recovery

Recovery involves more than avoiding substances. Steven Melemis describes five practical rules that encourage people to build a life that supports lasting change.

These rules are guiding ideas, not a test of a client’s character. Your CDCA role is to help clients explore how the ideas fit their circumstances, identify realistic next steps, and involve their treatment team when needed.

Maria’s reminder

“Clients don’t need a perfect recovery routine. We can help them identify one useful change at a time.”

Five Rules, Everyday Decisions

🌱1. Change Your Life

Develop routines, relationships, and environments that support recovery instead of relying only on avoiding substances.

🗣️2. Be Completely Honest

Practice honesty about cravings, setbacks, needs, and progress with the treatment team and trusted supports.

🤝3. Ask for Help

Reach out before risk escalates; make support-seeking a normal part of recovery.

🧡4. Practice Self-Care

Attend to physical health, sleep, nutrition, stress, and emotional well-being.

🧭5. Don’t Bend the Rules

Notice bargaining or exceptions that may increase risk; return to the coping plan and recovery commitments.

Small Choices Can Strengthen Recovery

The five rules work together. For example, asking for help may require honesty about a craving; changing a routine may make self-care easier; and noticing a tempting “exception” may be a signal to use the recovery plan.

📅Daily routine

A client schedules activities during evenings that used to involve substance use.

💬Honest check-in

A client tells the counselor that a stressful week has increased cravings.

📞Early support

A client contacts a sponsor or trusted support before entering a risky situation.

Maria’s reminder

“Instead of asking whether a client is following every rule, ask which idea could help with the challenge in front of them.”

Recovery Needs Change Over Time

Recovery is not just about stopping substance use. Over time, people may also focus on rebuilding relationships, strengthening life skills, and creating a meaningful future.

Your notes describe abstinence, repair, growth, and later recovery as a framework for thinking about changing needs. These are not rigid deadlines or a required sequence: people may revisit earlier challenges, and everyone's recovery timeline differs.

Maria's perspective

“Instead of asking how far along someone should be, ask what matters most to them right now and what support will help.”

Abstinence, Repair, Growth, and Later Recovery

🛑Abstinence

Focus on reducing or stopping substance use, responding to cravings, building supports, and coping with withdrawal-related challenges with appropriate clinical care.

🤝Repair

Address difficulties connected to substance use, rebuild trust where possible, work on relationships, and strengthen stability in daily life.

🌱Growth

Develop new skills, identities, goals, and meaningful activities that support life beyond substance use.

🧭Later Recovery

Maintain protective routines, notice new stressors and complacency, and continue adapting recovery supports when life circumstances change.

🔄Not a Straight Line

Clients can work on more than one area at once. Progress, setbacks, and new life events may change what is needed.

💬Individualized Support

Listen to client priorities, reinforce existing treatment goals, and communicate concerns to the qualified treatment team.

The approximate year ranges in some recovery models describe a proposed pattern, not a timetable to impose on clients or use to judge their progress.

Different Priorities, Different Supports

🧩Stabilizing Daily Life

Clients may need help accessing appointments, planning safe routines, identifying cravings, and contacting recovery supports.

🏠Repairing Connections

Some clients want help rebuilding relationships, reconnecting with family, or improving communication; timing and safety matter.

🎯Building a Future

Others may prioritize education, employment, interests, personal growth, or community involvement.

Within your CDCA role

“We can encourage strengths and help clients follow established plans, but we do not independently diagnose, determine treatment stages, or change a treatment plan.”

Support the Person, Not a Timeline

👂Listen

Ask which goals, strengths, barriers, and life changes matter to the individual client.

🧰Adapt Supports

Recovery priorities can shift among stabilization, repairing connections, personal growth, and ongoing maintenance.

🌤️Keep Prevention in View

Recovery can continue to need attention at any stage, especially during stressful or unfamiliar transitions.

Maria's reminder

“Recovery is personal. We support clients by recognizing their goals and needs, not by assigning deadlines to their progress.”
